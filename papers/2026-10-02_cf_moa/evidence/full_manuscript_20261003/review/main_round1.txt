CF-MoA: Taxonomy-Guided Benchmarking and Agent
Orchestration for Counterfactual Medical Reasoning
Anonymous Authors
Abstract
Medical question answering must respond selectively to changing evidence: a new ﬁnding can justify an ad-
ditional treatment, invalidate an earlier recommendation, reorder competing diagnoses, or leave the relevant
conclusion unchanged. Existing evaluations distribute these behaviors across datasets with diﬀerent tasks and
output formats, making their shared failure modes diﬃcult to identify. We introduce a ﬁve-relation functional
taxonomy and construct a benchmark of 6,104 units and 13,000 target judgments from 11 sources, retaining
native task interfaces with frozen source-speciﬁc scoring. An evaluation of seven medical question-answering
conﬁgurations reveals a consistent gap between overall accuracy and evidence-sensitive updating. Guided by
this diagnosis, we develop CF-MoA, a mixture of ﬁve functional agent roles for support addition, support
removal, candidate comparison, model-based consequence calculation, and stable readout. A sparse controller
connects these operations to existing systems. Across seven conﬁgurations, the corresponding heads improve
all 21 comparisons on the three support-update and candidate-comparison categories. In the full benchmark
evaluation, CF-MoA raises the aggregate score of two MedRAG conﬁgurations from 48.20 to 58.29 and from
47.35 to 59.36, while increasing correctness on both answer-changing and answer-preserving pairs. Component
controls identify explicit support execution and catalog-based re-decision as useful mechanisms. Together, these
results establish a functional framework for diagnosing and improving counterfactual medical reasoning across
heterogeneous tasks.

Introduction
A medical answer is useful only in relation to the ev-
idence that supports it.
When a patient’s condition
changes, a system may need to add a newly indicated
treatment, withdraw an option whose prerequisites no
longer hold, or reconsider the leading diagnosis. Other
changes should leave the answer intact. These behav-
iors require more than producing a plausible response to
each question in isolation: the system must track how
the changed information bears on the particular judg-
ment being requested.
Consider a treatment supported by two independent
indications.
Removing one indication does not neces-
sarily justify withdrawing the treatment; conversely,
preserving an answer after its only supporting condition
disappears is an error.
A diagnostic update presents
a related but diﬀerent problem.
New evidence can
strengthen a candidate without making it the most
likely diagnosis.
Grouping examples only by whether
their ﬁnal answer changes loses these distinctions. The
underlying operation—adding support, removing sup-
port, or comparing alternatives—is a more informative
basis for analysis.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives medi-
cal language models access to external knowledge, and
agent-based systems add iterative retrieval, multiple
answers, or explicit control of reasoning (Xiong et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Jiang et al.,
2025). Yet an expanded evidence context does not by
itself specify how support should be updated. Existing
counterfactual, perturbation, and robustness resources
provide complementary tests of this ability, but diﬀer in
their units, answer spaces, and evaluation rules. A com-
mon behavioral vocabulary is needed to connect their
observations without replacing their original tasks.
We address this problem through a taxonomy that de-
scribes the functional relation between a change and a
target judgment. Its ﬁve categories cover support ad-
dition, support removal, candidate comparison, conse-
quence calculation, and target preservation. The cate-
gories can overlap: the same change may add evidence
for a candidate and require a new comparison among al-
ternatives. We use this taxonomy to organize an inven-
tory of 26 candidate resources and construct a bench-
mark spanning 11 sources.
Its 6,104 evaluation units
retain the native questions, options, target deﬁnitions,
and source-speciﬁc scoring deﬁnitions.
The resulting evaluation reveals structure that an
overall score obscures.
Seven conﬁgurations from six
system families achieve aggregate scores between 47.35
and 51.61, but their scores on support removal range
from 7.59 to 41.77.
The strongest aggregate baseline
is not the strongest system on every functional cate-
gory.
This motivates an operation-centered approach:
use the observed failure mode to identify the missing
computation, then attach a component that performs
that computation through the existing interface.
We organize these components as CF-MoA, a mix-
ture of ﬁve functional agents. Its support agents execute
three-valued rules, its comparison agent makes an ex-
plicit decision over a diagnosis catalog, its consequence
1


agent applies local interventions to a supplied executable
model, and its preservation agent stabilizes native an-
swers through structured readout. A sparse controller
selects the applicable operation from the visible input
and available capabilities. This design gives each agent
a concrete responsibility and allows compatible opera-
tions to share an implementation.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce
a functional taxonomy and a provenance-preserving
benchmark that connect diverse medical counterfactual
and robustness tasks.
Second, we provide a seven-
conﬁguration diagnostic evaluation, together with head-
level comparisons that improve all 21 system–category
combinations on support addition, support removal,
and candidate comparison. Third, we develop CF-MoA
and evaluate the complete adopted controller on two
MedRAG conﬁgurations, obtaining aggregate gains of
10.09 and 12.01 percentage points and improved correct-
ness on both changing and preserving pairs. The accom-
panying controls explain which operations account for
these improvements and when further aggregation adds
little value.

Related Work
Behavioral evaluation and counterfactual tests.
Behavioral testing organizes evaluation around capa-
bilities and expected responses to controlled changes.
CheckList combines linguistic capabilities with minimum-
functionality, invariance, and directional tests (Ribeiro
et al., 2020). Contrast sets probe local decision bound-
aries through small, meaningful edits that typically
change the reference label (Gardner et al., 2020). Dyn-
abench develops this diagnostic perspective through
human-and-model-in-the-loop
data
collection
(Kiela
et al., 2021). These approaches motivate evaluating the
relationship between an input change and its required
response. Our ﬁve-category taxonomy applies that per-
spective to medical tasks: it records the changed condi-
tion, the requested decision, and the evidence needed to
justify revision or preservation. Categories can overlap
when one case requires several operations. This gives a
shared interpretation to tests drawn from sources with
diﬀerent task formats.
Medical counterfactual and robustness bench-
marks.
Medical datasets expose several distinct con-
sequences of perturbing an input.
MedEinst changes
discriminative evidence to test diﬀerential diagnosis
(Chen et al., 2026), while MedPIC-Bench provides
patient-speciﬁc medication questions with counterfac-
tual task families (MedPIC-Bench). HIV treatment sce-
narios examine causal consistency across interventions,
interactions, and side eﬀects (Prosperi et al., 2026).
Counterfactual Patient Variations and DiversityMedQA
examine demographic sensitivity in clinical decisions
(Benkirane et al., 2025; Rawat et al., 2024); cultural-cue
perturbations and AMQA extend this line to cultural
context and adversarial medical questions (Rezaei and
Shakeri, 2026; Xiao et al., 2025).
MedPerturb varies
gender cues, language style, and presentation format
(Gourabathina et al., 2025).
MedCounterFact studies
how models follow supplied medical evidence when its
content conﬂicts with plausibility or safety (Mo et al.,
2026).
MedRGB and BioRAB assess retrieval condi-
tions, including misleading or counterfactual evidence
(Ngo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025).
We bring eligible
instances from these eleven sources into one benchmark
while preserving their native questions, answer spaces,
and reference relations. The resulting functional labels
connect heterogeneous perturbations to the reasoning
operation that each instance actually tests.
Medical retrieval and generation.
Retrieval-augmented
generation combines a language model with exter-
nal information (Lewis et al., 2020).
MIRAGE and
the MedRAG toolkit examine how corpora, retrievers,
and language models aﬀect medical question answering
(Xiong et al., 2024). MedRGAG connects retrieved and
parametric knowledge (Li et al., 2026); MA-RAG uses
multiple rounds of agentic retrieval and reasoning (Wu
et al., 2026).
MedGENIE supplies generated contexts
for medical questions (Frisoni et al., 2024), and TC-RAG
maintains a memory stack to control iterative retrieval
(Jiang et al., 2025).
These methods provide comple-
mentary ways to acquire and organize knowledge. Our
seven evaluated conﬁgurations use them to diagnose
which functional requirements remain diﬃcult, then test
operation-speciﬁc heads on the resulting outputs and
admissible evidence. This connects system-level evalu-
ation to targeted repairs such as recalculating support
and comparing diagnostic candidates.
Aggregation and specialized agents.
Self-consistency
aggregates answers across sampled reasoning paths
(Wang et al., 2023), while Mixture-of-Agents passes mul-
tiple agents’ outputs between successive layers (Wang
et al., 2025). Contextual calibration addresses prompt-
dependent answer preferences (Zhao et al., 2021). These
ideas motivate distinct controls for additional sampling,
candidate comparison, and aggregation. CF-MoA gives
its ﬁve agents explicit functional responsibilities and
combines their outputs through a shared input contract
and routing interface. Our evaluation follows both the
gains from integrating these specialized operations and
the incremental eﬀects of candidate veriﬁcation, tying
each system change to the behavior it is intended to
improve.

A Functional Benchmark for
Medical
Counterfactual
Rea-
soning
We organize medical counterfactual tasks around the re-
lationship between a change and a speciﬁed judgment.
A comparison unit links a source-provided variant to its
reference or identiﬁes an explicit within-question hypo-
thetical. A target judgment identiﬁes what is being as-
sessed within that unit.
This distinction lets a single
clinical narrative support several functional annotations
while retaining the source’s original question and an-
swer contract. The resulting taxonomy covers condition
changes, hypothetical evidence, and perturbations whose
eﬀects are evaluated through native task performance.
3.1
Five overlapping functional relations
R1: adding support. A change introduces identiﬁ-
able evidence for a target judgment. For example, newly
reported wheezing can support a bronchitis candidate
without establishing it as the unique diagnosis.
R2:
withdrawing support.
An identiﬁable basis for a
judgment becomes invalid; the annotation states which
basis is lost and preserves any independent support.
R3:
comparing alternatives.
A change requires
reassessing the relative support for at least two identi-
ﬁable candidates.
Replacing a history of spontaneous
pneumothorax with deep-vein thrombosis illustrates a
change relevant to the comparison between pneumoth-
orax and pulmonary embolism in MedEinst Chen et al.
(2026).
R4:
deriving consequences.
A changed
premise requires propagating an established relationship
to an outcome not directly supplied. R5: preserving a
judgment. A real change leaves the evidence relevant
to a speciﬁed target intact; the annotation gives a reason
for that invariance.
These relations overlap because they describe func-
tions at the target level.
The same observation may
add support to one diagnosis and alter its comparison
with another. Annotation therefore records the change,
target, applicable relations, supporting text, and con-
clusions that cannot be inferred, then takes the union of
target labels for the parent unit. Omission of a symptom
does not establish its explicit absence, and an unchanged
answer alone does not establish invariance. Native com-
parison axes are preserved: an internal hypothetical and
an externally edited patient attribute receive separate
treatment when they address diﬀerent judgments.
3.2
Collection, qualiﬁcation, and sam-
pling
We registered 26 candidate sources and obtained 15,
comprising 14 text resources and one multimodal meta-
data release lacking required images. Their normalized
archive contains 55,991 units.
Source acquisition, se-
mantic classiﬁcation, and scoring eligibility are recorded
separately. Qualiﬁcation requires the source materials
needed to answer the native task and an established
scoring interface. This process yielded an eligible pool
of 38,123 units and 77,781 target judgments from 11
sources.
The included resources cover diagnostic and
prescribing edits Chen et al. (2026); MedPIC-Bench,
treatment hypotheticals Prosperi et al. (2026), demo-
graphic and cultural perturbations Benkirane et al.
(2025); Rezaei and Shakeri (2026); Xiao et al. (2025);
Rawat et al. (2024), clinical-service judgments Goura-
bathina et al. (2025), hypothetical research evidence Mo
et al. (2026), and evidence robustness Ngo et al. (2024);
Li et al. (2025).
The construction pipeline preserves provenance and
explicit correction history. Review distinguishes whether
a functional relation holds from whether its reference an-
swer supports scoring. For example, a valid invariance
annotation can remain while an unreliable reference is
excluded from scoring. Targeted review corrected 172
judgments across 130 units; these corrections are re-
tained alongside their earlier versions. Unit identiﬁers,
source locations, eligibility decisions, and target-to-input
mappings make the ﬁnal evaluation traceable to the ac-
quired materials.
The frozen v5 evaluation retains all eligible R1–R4
targets and samples 450 R5 units without replacement
from each of eight sources. Sampling uses sorted source-
speciﬁc identiﬁers and a ﬁxed seeded procedure, with-
out consulting model predictions. Original semantic la-
bels remain available, while a separate evaluation-label
ﬁeld speciﬁes membership in this evaluation. This yields
6,104 unique comparison units, with category counts of
2,866/79/733/13/3,600 for R1–R5. Category member-
ships overlap; in particular, all 733 R3 units also belong
to R1.
Equal R5 allocations preserve source coverage
while reducing the much larger invariance pool.

4
CF-MoA: Sparse Orchestration
of Five Functional Roles
CF-MoA turns the benchmark’s functional perspective
into ﬁve executable agent roles: adding support (A1),
withdrawing support (A2), comparing candidates (A3),
deriving consequences (A4), and preserving task-relevant
judgments (A5). The roles correspond to R1–R5 as de-
sign objectives; online execution depends on the current
input’s capabilities. Agents can share model weights and
deterministic components. The adopted conﬁguration,
denoted B, selects an applicable operation and returns
a native answer, as shown in Figure 1. Its organizing
principle is sparse specialization, with the base system
supplying the current answer and its actual inference
context.
4.1
Capability routing and native read-
out
Routing follows a ﬁxed priority: diagnosis-format inputs
enter A3; eligible rule tasks enter the shared A1/A2 ker-
nel; inputs providing a complete executable model enter
A4; supported single-choice, robustness, or relation tasks
with saved original messages enter A5. Other inputs re-
tain the base response. The router uses task format, vis-
ible options, question structure, and available computa-
tional resources. Source identiﬁers, benchmark relation
labels, gold answers, and paired endpoints are excluded.
Thus a component developed on one functional subset
can also operate on compatible inputs elsewhere in the
benchmark.
This interface gives each role a concrete applicabil-
ity domain: ﬁnite prescribing rules for A1/A2, a ﬁxed
diagnosis catalog for A3, a supplied executable mecha-
nism and state for A4, and supported native decision for-
mats for A5. In the complete v5 evaluation, each back-
bone routes 3,560 inputs to A3, 123 to A1/A2, 10,178
to A5, and 44 to passthrough.
A4 has no qualifying
clinical v5 input; its supplied-model evaluation is re-
ported separately.
The controller records the selected
route, requests, operation traces, and complete returned
response.
4.2
A1/A2: scoped support recomputa-
tion
A1 and A2 share a kernel containing 71 manually en-
coded public prescribing rules. Eligibility checks require
a supported multiple-choice format, an age of at least 65
in the implemented question pattern, and a recognized
medication option. The kernel extracts the requested ac-
tion and scope, such as avoidance or renal dose adjust-
ment. One call to the original backbone extracts only
the required patient facts from the numbered current
question, with evidence sentence identiﬁers. It does not
receive the base answer or retrieved documents during
this extraction. Missing or rejected evidence produces
an unknown value.
The executor evaluates conditions, exceptions, and
independent support paths using met, contradicted,
and unknown.
Conjunction combines requirements
within a path; disjunction preserves independent paths.
Consequently, invalidating one risk does not remove an
option supported by another satisﬁed risk. Let S0 be the
initial valid option set and let M, C, U partition options
by their aggregate status. A1 adds M \ S0; A2 removes
C ∩S0. For ordinary valid sets, the result is
S⋆= M ∪(S0 ∩U).
(2)
The native assembler additionally handles exclusive
“None” options, invalid initial answers, and empty out-
puts. Both roles therefore use one fact extraction and
one execution, while retaining separate addition and
withdrawal traces.
4.3
A3:
catalog-based candidate com-
parison
A3 uses all 49 disease names in the public DDXPlus cat-
alog Fansi Tchango et al. (2022). The same backbone
reads the current patient and the base system’s actual
context, with diagnoses bound to scoreable codes under
six ﬁxed catalog orders. For each order, it subtracts a
content-free log-score prior from the patient-conditioned
4


code score, following the general calibration principle
of separating contextual evidence from answer prefer-
ences Zhao et al. (2021). The prior is the logarithm of
the mean of three content-free candidate distributions,
computed separately for each order; its subtraction co-
eﬃcient is ﬁxed at one. A3 selects the highest corrected
score per order and votes across orders, breaking vote
ties using average corrected probabilities. The selected
catalog entry is mapped back to the native diagnosis.
The catalog and scorer’s frozen normalization are sep-
arate resources; this decision stage changes candidate
selection without expanding the scoring dictionary.
4.4
A4: executing a local intervention
A4 compiles an intervention into a typed local patch
when the input supplies the executable model, parame-
ters, complete initial state, and factual action schedule.
Unmodiﬁed actions remain intact. The numerical execu-
tor runs factual and patched trajectories from the same
starting state and projects their consequences onto the
requested time and outcome. Our implementation uses
the public glucose–insulin simulator Xie (2025). Local
patches reduce the need to regenerate a complete ac-
tion plan, allowing intervention ﬁdelity to be evaluated
against a same-tool full-plan control. The returned con-
sequence is computed from the supplied mechanism and
executed actions.
4.5
A5: task-adapted robust readout
A5 retains the adopted component F. For ordinary sup-
ported tasks, F preserves the complete original messages
and samples up to ﬁve native responses at temperature
0.7, seeds 42–46, and a 2,048-token output limit. It votes
over valid answer keys, stops once one key receives three
votes, and returns the earliest complete response carry-
ing a winning key. Final vote ties use earliest occurrence;
if no vote is valid, the ﬁrst response remains invalid. This
is a native-output adaptation of sampling and answer ag-
gregation Wang et al. (2023).
For explicit premise–hypothesis tasks with positive/negative
options, F uses a natural language inference (NLI)
branch.
It scores both A/B label mappings directly,
generates one current-question analysis, and scores both
mappings again conditioned on that analysis.
Each
two-label distribution is normalized before the four se-
mantic distributions are averaged.
The highest mean
determines the answer; exact ties follow native option
order. A ﬁnal model call generates the complete native
response with its answer ﬁeld constrained to that deci-
sion. This path uses four one-token score callbacks and
two complete generations at temperature zero, preserv-
ing the original evidence and required auxiliary ﬁelds.
Missing code scores require a matching score request
and remain visible in the cost ledger.

Experimental Setup
Seven system conﬁgurations.
We evaluate direct
answering with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Dubey et al.,
2024); an all-Llama implementation of MedRGAG; in-
trinsic MA-RAG with Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025);
MedRAG with each of these two backbones; MedGE-
NIE with Llama; and TC-RAG with Qwen. These are
seven conﬁgurations from six system families. We refer
to the two MedRAG conﬁgurations as MedRAG-Llama
and MedRAG-Qwen.
Their selected versions include
structured output handling for MedRAG-Llama, the
second local prompt version of MedGENIE, and typed
control for TC-RAG. MA-RAG uses eight solvers per
round, up to four rounds, with early agreement stop-
ping. Its available retrieval collection contains PubMed,
textbooks, and Wikipedia; the other retrieval conﬁg-
urations have their own corpus and retrieval settings.
Appendix D records the implementation identities and
reuse accounting. The comparison measures the com-
plete conﬁgured systems; it does not equalize all retrieval
and inference budgets.
Head and system evaluations.
We separate two
experimental scopes. The head evaluation attaches the
support-update and comparison operations to the saved
contexts and original answers of all seven conﬁgurations.
It covers all 2,866 support-addition units, 79 support-
removal units, and 733 candidate-comparison units. The
clinical categories overlap, as speciﬁed in Section 3. Di-
agnosis heads use the corresponding system contexts;
the rule branches reuse two backbone-speciﬁc sets of
extracted facts and combine them oﬄine with each
system’s original answer.
The subsequent full-system
evaluation executes the adopted CF-MoA controller,
denoted B, on MedRAG-Llama and MedRAG-Qwen.
Each conﬁguration covers the same 13,905 unique in-
puts and 15,616 native scoring mappings. The earlier
head outputs and the later full-system outputs are re-
ported separately.
Mechanism controls.
We compare catalog-based di-
agnosis with its original answer, one catalog decision,
six uncalibrated decisions, and calibrated decisions on
the 680-unit diagnostic subset. A 123-input control re-
moves the addition or removal operation while retain-
ing the same extracted facts and rule statuses. Model-
based consequence execution is evaluated separately on
24 virtual proﬁles with 12 questions each. For the full
system, we also evaluate three candidate-selection vari-
ants and an expanded sampling pool; their six-arm de-
sign and complete results appear in Appendix C. These
controls distinguish a useful component from a beneﬁt
attributable to extra sampling alone.

correctly while struggling to revise the support for a spe-
ciﬁc decision.
The taxonomy makes these diﬀerences actionable.
Support removal calls for checking whether an answer’s
prerequisites still hold; diagnosis calls for comparing
viable candidates under the changed evidence. Neither
need is fully described by the number of retrieved pas-
sages or generated answers. This distinction motivates
the specialized operations evaluated next.

7
Discussion and Conclusion
The experiments connect three levels of analysis:
a
functional taxonomy, operations that address its failure
modes, and a controller that composes those operations
through existing medical question-answering interfaces.
The taxonomy reveals weaknesses hidden by the aggre-
gate ranking. The head evaluation shows that several
of these weaknesses recur across diﬀerent upstream sys-
tems.
The full controller then turns the specialized
operations into a usable inference procedure, improving
both aggregate accuracy and correctness across related
inputs.
The component controls suggest a practical design
principle: make the required decision explicit before in-
creasing the amount of generated reasoning. A catalog
makes competing diagnoses available for direct com-
parison. A support executor turns uncertain narrative
revision into option-level checks with a deﬁned treat-
ment of unknown facts. An executable model turns an
intervention speciﬁcation into a veriﬁable calculation.
These mechanisms diﬀer, but each narrows the gap be-
tween the behavior requested by the task and the action
performed by the system.
The results also clarify the role of a mixture of agents.
In CF-MoA, the agents are deﬁned by the operations
they perform, with shared weights and shared execution
where appropriate. The useful combination is selective:
a rule question beneﬁts from support execution, while a
9


diagnostic question beneﬁts from candidate comparison.
The mixed results from additional veriﬁcation and gen-
eration show that assigning more agents to every input
is not suﬃcient to improve the system.
Overall, this work provides a benchmark and an op-
erational framework for studying how medical language
systems should respond to changed conditions.
The
11-source benchmark, seven-conﬁguration diagnosis, 21
head comparisons, and two full-system evaluations form
a connected empirical account.
Further progress can
be measured at both levels:
whether each operation
generalizes to new cases, and whether their controller
improves correct change and correct preservation to-
gether.
Limitations
The benchmark and component panels were used dur-
ing development. The bootstrap intervals quantify un-
certainty within these evaluated families; a separate
family-disjoint evaluation after ﬁnal controller selection
is needed to estimate performance under independent
deployment conditions. The ﬁnal controller was evalu-
ated on two MedRAG conﬁgurations, while transfer to
seven systems was established for the earlier support
and comparison heads.
Source coverage and category balance also matter.
Support removal contains 79 units and consequence
calculation contains 13; category labels overlap, and a
substantial part of the preservation subset comes from
perturbation and retrieval-robustness tasks. The taxon-
omy was applied through structured review and source-
speciﬁc checks, but independent clinician agreement on
its assignments has not been measured. Source labels
and rubrics inherit the strengths and limitations of their
original datasets.
Several components have explicit resource assump-
tions.
The rule executor depends on its 71-rule bank
and extracted facts, and diagnosis uses a ﬁnite public
catalog. Consequently, part of the observed gain con-
cerns candidate coverage and readable output, rather
than an isolated improvement in latent clinical reason-
ing. The consequence agent was validated on a supplied
physiological model and virtual proﬁles. Baselines have
diﬀerent retrieval, formatting, and inference budgets,
and a complete resource-matched comparison remains
open. These qualiﬁcations deﬁne the settings in which
the reported evidence applies.
Ethical Considerations
The study evaluates existing research datasets and sim-
ulated proﬁles. It does not establish clinical eﬀectiveness
or authorize patient-care use. Dataset provenance and
original access conditions must accompany any redistri-
bution. Evaluation artifacts retain source task contracts,
including failures and invalid outputs, so that down-
stream users can inspect the system’s behavior rather
than relying only on aggregate scores. Author and data-
availability declarations appear in Appendix E.

Benchmark Composition and Annotation
A.1
Source inventory and qualiﬁcation
Table 3 gives the exact composition of the frozen evaluation. Source citations and acquisition records accompany the
source manifest. The original inventory contained 26 candidates, of which 15 were acquired. Four acquired resources
did not contribute eligible v5 targets: the clinical laboratory and EquityMedQA resources required additional answer
or rubric adjudication; MedCF required an editing operation outside the implemented answering protocol; and
MedMKEB lacked the required images. For the other 11 candidates, the speciﬁed ﬁxed cases, counterfactual edits,
or required restricted context had not been obtained at collection time. These acquisition statuses describe the
archived study materials.
The 55,991 archived units comprise 53,494 text units and 2,497 metadata units with missing images. An initial
review frame contained 28,243 units and 80,910 judgments. Its review ledger records 603 individual semantic reviews,
13,464 grouped semantic reviews, and 14,176 structural checks. Subsequent completion and eligibility review covered
all archived units, producing 109,599 reviewed judgments, of which 77,781 were eligible in 38,123 units. The evaluation
sample is drawn from this completed pool rather than from the initial capped frame.
A.2
Annotation examples and overlap
An annotation identiﬁes the source comparison axis before assigning a relation. In a MedEinst case, the addition of
wheezing supports a bronchitis candidate (R1), while the associated diagnostic question can require comparison with
acute laryngitis (R3). A DiversityMedQA sex-attribute change removes one demographic basis for a Lesch–Nyhan
candidate (R2), while other ﬁndings remain available. In CPV-MedQA, changing an ethnicity description leaves the
chemotherapy and ototoxicity evidence relevant to a pharmacological question intact (R5). These examples illustrate
functional labels on speciﬁed targets, rather than rules for assigning a diagnosis from a single feature.
Labels are assigned to targets and then combined at the unit level. In v5, R3 is contained in R1; R1 and R5
share 450 units, R1 and R2 share four, and R2 and R3 share three. R1–R3 contain 2,941 distinct units. The sum
of category memberships is 7,291, whereas the union used for ALL contains 6,104 units. The 13,000 selected target
judgments, 13,905 distinct inputs, and 15,616 scoring mappings refer to diﬀerent representations of these same tasks.
Of the scoring mappings, 6,818 address reference endpoints and 8,798 address non-reference endpoints.

A.3
Sampling and version lineage
The earlier v2 evaluation contained 3,105 units and eight sources. A v3 display assigned overlapping units to a single
category for presentation, without changing their answers. The completed v4 pool restored multi-label evaluation
and expanded eligibility review. The v5 evaluation used in this paper retains all eligible R1–R4 targets and samples
450 R5 units from each of eight sources. These source samples are drawn without replacement from sorted identiﬁers
using a source-speciﬁc seeded procedure. The original semantic labels and the selected evaluation labels are stored
separately. Neither prior predictions nor ﬁnal method scores determine sample membership.
The 450-unit allocation balances source representation within R5; it does not assert equal numbers of independent
patients or equal sampling probabilities across sources. Related variants remain linked to their parent families for
paired analysis and bootstrap resampling. The source manifest, membership ﬁles, and scoring mappings are the
authoritative deﬁnition of the evaluated sample.

Complete head comparisons

B.2
Diagnosis: catalog, calibration, and source checks
The public catalog contains 49 diagnoses, while the frozen task scorer recognizes 46 normalized diagnosis keys. The
catalog is supplied to the decision component as a ﬁxed resource; it is not constructed from the current item’s gold
answer. On 680 diagnostic units, Table 5(a) separates the single-catalog improvement from repeated selection and
calibration. Across repaired R3 outcomes, 1,186 of 1,272 previously failed outputs were unmapped diagnoses, 23 were
otherwise invalid, and 63 were valid wrong answers. This breakdown indicates that output grounding accounts for a
large part of the measured improvement; unmapped outputs are not retrospectively counted as clinically correct.
Additional controls explored alternative name/code presentations on 40 diagnostic inputs. Direct code selection
with a 384-token budget obtained 18/21 correct for Llama/Qwen; direct name selection at a 128-token budget
obtained 15/18. Reasoning with names at 384 tokens obtained 18/19, while reasoning with codes at that budget
obtained 21/22. Because the direct-name and direct-code controls used diﬀerent budgets, their diﬀerence does not
isolate the eﬀect of codes. The complete run identities and budgets are retained in the evidence ledger.
Two additional source checks assessed catalog compatibility. On 47 ASCENT records, the calibrated head obtained
38/39 correct for Llama/Qwen, compared with 39/39 for uncalibrated voting and 38/39 for a single catalog decision.
These records included 42 source-training and ﬁve source-test examples and subsequently informed development.
Across three CUPCase batches totaling 18 records, the calibrated head obtained 15/16, uncalibrated voting 16/17,
single selection 15/18, and character-normalized original outputs 15/14. The checks support useful catalog-based
selection on additional records, while providing no consistent incremental advantage for calibration.

Support removal and remaining support
The constant “None” control in Table 5(b) uses the same 55-input rule coverage and retains the same 24 original
answers outside coverage. Forty-six covered targets have “None” as their answer; the other nine retain support
for at least one option. On those nine, original correct counts for Direct, MedRGAG, MA-RAG, MedRAG-Llama,
MedRAG-Qwen, MedGENIE, and TC-RAG are 5/4/1/3/0/2/3, and head counts are 6/4/3/4/6/5/2. This subset
measures the ability to retain independent support after another basis is removed.
The 123-input add/remove ablation in the main text holds the extracted facts and statuses ﬁxed. It evaluates
the shared executor on the complete rule-input panel used by the controller, rather than on the 79-unit R2 category
alone. The full executor leaves one Llama input and 14 Qwen inputs unavailable; these remain included in the
123-input denominator. The contrast therefore tests the eﬀect of the speciﬁed operations under the same upstream
extraction.
B.4
Supplied-model consequence execution
The separate model panel uses 24 virtual parameter and initial-state proﬁles, each with 12 questions. A question
supplies the physiological mechanism, factual schedule, requested local intervention, and output projection. The
typed-patch procedure edits the named action and executes the resulting trajectory. The full-plan control has the
same tool access but regenerates a complete plan; the no-intervention control retains patch structure while omitting
the actual intervention. Table 6 reports binary, numerical, and full-output correctness. The 288 questions represent
structured model queries, not 288 independent patients.
B.5
Stable readout and generated aggregation
The F component panel comprises an initial 64 groups, an additional 128 groups, and a ﬁnal frozen extension of 16
groups. Group-weighted paired scores for the complete 208-group panel are reported in Section 6.4. The ordinary
branch aggregates valid native answer keys; the NLI branch combines direct and analysis-conditioned semantic
probabilities across both label mappings. The component’s exact stopping, tie-breaking, and invalid-output policies
are speciﬁed in Section 4 and the accompanying implementation.
The development study also tested generated aggregation over the available expert outputs on 121 inputs. The
adopted controller obtained 87/82 correct for Llama/Qwen, a three-draw alternative 84/74, and the default generated
aggregator 75/73. Adding a newly developed support-addition proposal to the candidate pool produced 72/57 under
top-one selection and 79/74 under generated aggregation. These experiments led to the shared support kernel and
sparse adopted controller. The 121-input panel is a development control and is not added to the full-v5 evaluation

C.3
Candidate veriﬁcation and alternative selection
For an ordinary F input with at least two distinct valid answer keys, we retain the earliest complete saved response
representing each key. C2 scores whether each candidate is correct under two opposite A/B label assignments. C3
uses the same procedure and includes the saved candidate rationale as model-generated reasoning to be checked. For
candidate k, the veriﬁer averages its normalized probability of correctness under the two assignments:
v(k) = 1
2 [pAB(correct | x, k, rk) + pBA(correct | x, k, rk)] .
(3)
The rationale rk is omitted in C2. The highest-scoring candidate is selected; exact ties favor the saved F answer, then
native order. The complete existing representative is returned, preserving its required auxiliary ﬁelds. Non-ordinary
F inputs, single-candidate pools, and unavailable B outputs retain B. Technical veriﬁcation failure also falls back to
B and is recorded.
The full ledger identiﬁes 2,964 Llama and 2,295 Qwen ordinary disagreement inputs. Two Llama B outputs are
unavailable, leaving 2,962 actually veriﬁed Llama inputs. C3 records one Llama and eight Qwen technical veriﬁer
failures with fallback. C5 instead scores a joint ﬁnite choice over the same candidates and rationales. C4 adds three
predetermined native-answer draws to the old F pool and votes over the expanded pool; its unavailable-output policy
is reported separately, rather than replaced with the veriﬁer fallback.

D.2
Rebuilding paper artifacts
The paper package separates native LaTeX source, frozen numeric inputs, plotting scripts, and audit receipts. The

Running latex/build.sh compiles it using Tectonic or a local LATEX toolchain. No conversion from a word processor
or markup draft is required.
The reproduction scripts read frozen summaries and regenerate the main tables, source and format breakdowns,
and vector ﬁgures.
They retain source hashes and assert denominators and selected values before exporting re-
sults. The source package includes all assets required to compile the paper oﬄine once the TeX dependencies are

installed. Its companion evidence package identiﬁes the original conﬁguration, prediction, score, and trace artifacts
