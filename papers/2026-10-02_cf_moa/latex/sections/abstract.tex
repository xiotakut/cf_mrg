Medical question answering must respond selectively to changing evidence: a new finding can justify an additional treatment, invalidate an earlier recommendation, reorder competing diagnoses, or leave the relevant conclusion unchanged. Existing evaluations distribute these behaviors across datasets with different tasks and output formats, making their shared failure modes difficult to identify. We introduce a five-relation functional taxonomy and construct a benchmark of 6,104 units and 13,000 target judgments from 11 sources, retaining native task interfaces with frozen source-specific scoring. An evaluation of seven medical question-answering configurations reveals a consistent gap between overall accuracy and evidence-sensitive updating. Guided by this diagnosis, we develop a counterfactual mixture of agents (\cfmoa{}), with five functional roles for support addition, support removal, candidate comparison, model-based consequence calculation, and stable readout. A sparse controller connects these operations to existing systems. Across seven configurations, the corresponding heads improve all 21 comparisons on the three support-update and candidate-comparison categories. In the full benchmark evaluation, \cfmoa{} raises the aggregate score of two MedRAG configurations from 48.20 to 58.29 and from 47.35 to 59.36, while increasing correctness on both answer-changing and answer-preserving pairs. Component controls identify explicit support execution and catalog-based re-decision as useful mechanisms. Together, these results establish a functional framework for diagnosing and improving counterfactual medical reasoning across heterogeneous tasks.
